1 · Three terms, three layers

They aren't rivals. They nest.

A loop repeats one task until a check passes. A graph moves work between stages and checks. A factory is the whole system around both, including the people.

Software factory

stable idea

The whole delivery system: intake, spec, plan, build, verify, review, release, and learning from what happens in production. The word is decades old. The agentic version is new. Uber now calls its own setup a software factory.

Loop engineering

emerging term

Designing one bounded feedback loop: trigger, act, check, repair, then stop or escalate. IBM defined it in July 2026 , and a study a month later found 217 working loops in public repos.

Graph engineering

unsettled term

Designing how stages and loops connect: branches, retries, checkpoints, approvals. Only one paper uses it this way so far , and "graph" also means knowledge graphs, like Uber's 24-million-node context graph.

What to call things.Build around the parts that won't change: trigger, gate, retry policy, state, budget, escalation. The labels may not last.

The reports disagree here. Report B says neither "loop engineering" nor "graph engineering" has a traceable origin. That's wrong: both have dated 2026 sources, which report A found.

2 · Lineage

An old word with a new worker

Every version of the software factory tried to move know-how out of individual heads and into a repeatable system. What changed in 2026 is who does the work.

1969Hitachi sets up a software facility run explicitly as a factory.
1991Cusumano's Japan's Software Factories studies Hitachi, Toshiba, NEC and Fujitsu: standard process, reuse, automation and quality control. Whether a factory beats craft depends on the product and market.
2004Greenfield and Short's Software Factories: product lines, models, patterns and domain-specific languages. Industrialised tooling for people, not autonomy.
2025The pieces appear. Huntley's "Ralph" loop re-feeds one prompt to a coding agent. Anthropic writes up context engineering and harnesses for long-running agents. Spec Kit and Kiro make spec-driven development a product.
Feb 2026OpenAI's harness-engineering post: "Humans steer. Agents execute."
Jul–Aug 2026The vocabulary arrives: IBM's loop engineering explainer, the loop-adoption study, and the paper that splits graph, loop and harness engineering.
Aug 2026Uber publishes "Running a Software Factory Efficiently at Uber Scale", the most detailed public account of one.
"Dark factory" is a sales term.Lights-out, no-human software delivery is how some vendors describe themselves. There is no standard behind it and no independent evidence for it.
3 · Anatomy

Eight stages, each with a gate it can't talk its way past

Agents do the work inside each stage. Deterministic checks decide whether work moves on. Everything that matters is written down outside the model.

  1. 1 · IntakeTriage the requestgate: typed work item, risk classhuman: is this worth doing?
  2. 2 · SpecTurn intent into checkable behaviourgate: every criterion testable, no open questionshuman: ambiguous product calls
  3. 3 · PlanDecompose and pick the approachgate: architecture rules, size limithuman: irreversible architecture
  4. 4 · Build ↻Implement in an isolated worktreegate: build, types, lint, unit tests, attempt and cost capshuman: only on budget or ambiguity
  5. 5 · Verify ↻Run checks the builder didn't writegate: protected tests green, evidence completehuman: visual and UX quality
  6. 6 · ReviewChallenge the change against the specgate: findings resolved, test files untouchedhuman: high-risk judgement
  7. 7 · ReleaseMerge, deploy, watch the canarygate: merge queue, rollback readyhuman: every merge, at first
  8. 8 · LearnTurn escapes into new tests and rulesgate: each escaped defect gets a checkhuman: which lessons become policy
Durable statespec version · risk class · current stage · attempts · budget left · branch · gate results · evidence · decisions

↻ marks stages that loop. Failed verification and review findings go back to Build; production problems go back to Intake. The context window is a cache, not a database.

Seen in production: Dropbox Nova

Each agent session is isolated and wrapped in a durable workflow. Publishing code stays outside the agent's hands, one branch per session. Its flaky-test agent starts from real pass/fail logs, not guesses.

The stage contract

Every stage returns one of four answers, with evidence attached. That makes retries and escalations explicit instead of buried in chat history.

5 · Evidence

Who is actually running one

Strong evidence for factory parts and narrow loops. No independent evidence yet for a general, unattended factory.

Uber

first-party telemetry
  • More than 70% of pull requests are attributed to local or cloud agents.
  • More than 3,600 internal agent skills, run over 30,000 times a day.
  • Measures agents by outcome: cost per merged PR, review or alert, plus revert rate and time to recover. Benchmarks before switching models.
  • Loading 100+ MCP tools the usual way cost 50–70K tokens of schema per request, so they changed how tools load.
  • People still review and handle escalations. "Attributed to agents" doesn't mean "merged with no human edits".

Growth, Feb to Aug 2026

The 7× counts all employees, not just engineers.

OpenAI

first-party experiment

Built an internal product with no hand-written code, starting from an empty repo, in roughly a tenth of the time it estimated by hand. Its takeaway was the harness, not the model.

Stripe

first-party

Its "Minions" agents land over a thousand merged pull requests a week, and humans still review them.

Meta

narrow domain

KernelEvolve raised one ads model's inference throughput by over 60% on one GPU class, with kernels written in hours instead of weeks. It works because speed is measurable.

Spotify

telemetry + survey

Over 99% of engineers use AI tools weekly, 94% say it helps, and PR frequency rose 76%. Coding stopped being the bottleneck; review and coordination took its place.

Reality checks

  • On 115 real version-upgrade tasks across 17 repos, the best model resolved 39.1%. Long, multi-part change is still hard.
  • In METR's 2025 trial, experienced developers were about 19% slower with AI tools and believed they were faster.
  • DORA's 2025 report frames AI as an amplifier: more throughput, and more instability where the foundations are weak.
  • Nobody has published an independent figure for changes merged with zero human edits, or for cost per change against a human baseline.

7 · Failure modes

A factory multiplies whatever you put in it

Good tests get multiplied into tested code. Vague specs get multiplied into plausible wrong answers, faster than people could write them.

Runaway work

  • Retry spirals. The same failure, retried with growing context. Cap attempts, time and cost; escalate instead of trying harder.
  • Spinning in place. Code keeps changing, gate results don't. Stop after a few attempts with no measurable progress.
  • Review overload. PRs arrive faster than anyone can judge them. Limit work in progress.

Gaming the gate

  • Editing the oracle. The agent changes tests or fixtures so its code passes. Protect those files; separate who builds from who checks.
  • Self-review. The critic shares the author's assumptions. Deterministic checks first; independent reviewer second.
  • Scope drift. The agent solves a nearby, easier problem. Pin the spec version; limit which files it may touch.

Runtime and state

  • Lost state after a restart. A resumed run repeats or forgets work. Store state outside the model; checkpoint every transition.
  • Double side effects. A retried tool call runs twice. Use idempotency keys.
  • Deadlock. Agents wait on each other, or on an approval nobody gives. Deadlines, leases, an explicit blocked state.

Slow rot

  • Context bloat. Too many tools and docs loaded every turn. Load on demand.
  • Factory entropy. Skills and rules pile up and contradict each other. Version them, test them, delete stale ones.
  • Benchmark overfitting. Evals look great while production escapes rise. Rotate a hidden holdout set.
8 · Build path

Build it one level at a time

Start with one cell you can trust, prove it on replayed work, then connect cells into a graph. Add parallelism and scheduling only when the numbers say the last level holds.

AssistA person drives agent sessions.Exit: the repo is agent-readable
Verified loopOne agent retries one task until a hard gate passes or the budget runs out.Exit: replays stop correctly and never bypass the gate
Factory cellTrigger → spec → loop → evidence → a person merges.Exit: survives a crash without losing its place
Multi-stageExplicit intake, spec, plan, build, verify and review stages.Exit: every transition leaves evidence
Parallel lineSeveral workers in worktrees, one integration gate.Exit: more accepted work, no rise in reverts
Managed fleetScheduled runs, model routing, continuous evals.Exit: cost and quality per accepted outcome

The highlighted level is the smallest factory worth building. These levels are the reports' own synthesis, not an industry standard.

Hand out autonomy by risk, not all at once

RiskExampleStart with
LowFixtures, simple dependency bumpsAgent opens the PR; auto-merge once history earns it

HighAuth, money, schemas, securityA person approves the plan and the merge

First five moves

  1. Write the contracts before the agents. Stages pass typed state and return PASS, RETRY, ESCALATE or FAIL.
  2. Build an eval set first. 20–50 past changes: easy bugs, UI work, refactors, known nasty cases.
  3. Keep the outer graph boring. Plain code you can read before any framework.
  4. Add parallelism last. Worktrees isolate files, not meaning.
  5. Promote by risk class. Move people to the decisions where their judgement adds something.
9 · Open problems

What nobody has solved yet

The hard parts left aren't about model intelligence.

Checking intent

Tests prove behaviour, not that the right thing was built. Meta's result works because speed is measurable; most product decisions aren't.

Long-horizon change

Good issue-level coding doesn't add up to reliable work across dozens of files.

Independent outcomes

Plenty of first-party numbers; no cross-company study of which autonomy level keeps cost, defects and security in balance.

Coordination

Static graphs are predictable but rigid. Free-form conductors adapt but are hard to inspect. Nothing has won.

A factory that rewrites itself

Letting the system edit its own prompts, skills or gates raises the question of who checks the checker.

Where people belong

Approval everywhere kills throughput. No approval turns ambiguity into automated mistakes. The answer is per transition, by risk.
